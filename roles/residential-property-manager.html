How a residential property manager works

This person runs day-to-day operations for rental properties owned by others, acting under a management agreement that defines their authority, repair limits and fees. They handle the full tenancy cycle: marketing and screening, leasing, rent collection and delinquency, maintenance triage and vendor coordination, inspections, renewals, move-outs and evictions. They do this while following fair housing, deposit, notice and trust accounting laws, and keeping owners informed and tenants housed safely.

Also called: Residential Property Manager, Property Manager, Portfolio Manager, Leasing and Property Manager, Single-Family Rental Manager, Community Manager. 1,362 statements in this map.

Workflow steps 454 in the full map

  • A tenant submits a maintenance request through the portal in AppFolio, Buildium, or Rent Manager, which creates a work order tied to the unit and tenant.
  • The manager triages each new request into emergency, urgent, or routine, with emergencies handled within minutes and routine items within a few business days.
  • For a repair above the owner's limit, the manager gets a vendor estimate and emails or texts it to the owner for approval.
  • The manager sends a friendly reminder by text, email, or phone call to each delinquent tenant right after the grace period.
  • If rent stays unpaid, the manager serves a pay-or-quit or similar notice, the form and number of days depending on the state, often 3 to 14 days.
  • When the notice period expires without payment, the manager hands the file to the company's attorney or eviction service with the lease, ledger, and notice.

Pains 272 in the full map

  • After a loss, the manager must coordinate the mitigation company, adjuster, owner, and displaced tenant, all of whom move at different speeds.
  • A tenant leaving early leaves the owner with lost rent unless the unit is re-rented quickly, so the manager has to balance both parties.
  • Voucher unit inspections and approval can leave a unit empty for several weeks while the housing authority schedules, and the owner blames the manager for the vacancy.
  • Owners who previously self-managed hand over leases with missing clauses, handwritten changes, or undocumented deposits, which become the manager's problem.
  • An owner selling mid-lease means the manager handles buyer showings, deposit transfers, and tenant anxiety, then loses the management contract.
  • Many tenants never buy renters insurance, so a fire or leak turns into a dispute over who pays for their belongings.

Workarounds 91 in the full map

  • Managers keep a spreadsheet of lease expirations, inspection dates, and license renewals as a backup to the software's reminders.
  • Many managers ask for bank statements or use income verification services that connect to payroll or bank accounts, because pay stubs are easy to fake.
  • Managers use translation apps or a bilingual coworker for important calls and translate key notices informally, while sending the legal notice in its required form.

Constraints 262 in the full map

  • After-hours calls route to an answering service or a rotating on-call phone that a different staff member carries each week.
  • Each management agreement sets a repair approval limit, often 250 to 500 dollars, above which the owner must approve before work starts.
  • Vendors must have a current W-9, general liability insurance certificate, and often a license on file before they can be paid or dispatched.
  • State landlord-tenant law usually requires 24 to 48 hours written notice before entering a occupied unit for non-emergency work.
  • Managers keep a required reserve balance, such as 300 to 1,000 dollars per property, in the owner account so small repairs can be paid without waiting for a contribution.
  • Late fees are charged automatically or manually after the grace period in the lease, commonly the fifth of the month, subject to state caps.

Judgment calls 75 in the full map

  • Some states restrict charging late fees or require specific wording, so the manager checks the lease clause before posting a fee.
  • Calls the applicant's prior landlord, not only the current one, because a current landlord may say anything to get rid of a bad tenant.
  • For assistance animal requests with online-sourced documentation, the manager checks whether the letter shows a provider relationship before approving, without charging pet fees or pet rent.

Team dynamics 87 in the full map

  • Owners who self-managed before hiring the company hand over incomplete leases, missing deposit records and undocumented side agreements with tenants.

Limits 47 in the full map

  • Owners delay capital work to save money, and small problems turn into larger repairs, making the manager's reports harder.
  • Owners who live out of state or abroad can take days to approve a repair, leaving the manager in the middle between an angry tenant and an owner who will not answer.
  • Plumbing and HVAC vendors fill up quickly in heat waves and freezes, so emergency work orders wait in a queue the manager cannot shorten.

Improvisation 74 in the full map

  • The manager offers payment plans or cash-for-keys agreements in some cases when it is quicker and cheaper than completing an eviction.
  • Offers a written payment plan to a tenant with a temporary job loss, signed by both sides, when the owner agrees, to avoid the cost of court.

Questions worth asking people in this job

How often, how badly, and in which workplaces: ask the people who do the work.

  • Which maintenance problems come up most often in your portfolio, and which are seasonal? Shows where time, vendor capacity and budgets actually go.
  • How long do things really take from work order creation to completion for routine, urgent and emergency items? Actual timelines differ from stated targets and set tenant and owner expectations.
  • What is your actual owner approval limit, and how often do you act above it anyway? Reveals tolerated workarounds and risk in the authority structure.
  • How does your after-hours coverage work, and how many calls are real emergencies? Determines workload, burnout and dispatch decisions.
  • Which software do you use, and what tasks do you still do by spreadsheet or by hand? Shows tool gaps and manual workarounds.
  • How many doors do you personally manage, and what mix of houses and small buildings? Workload and complexity vary widely.
  • How do you handle owners who are slow to respond or refuse repairs? Informal escalation practices are rarely written down.
  • Where do eviction cases stall in your county, and how long do they take? Local court timelines drive owner communication and strategy.
  • When do you offer payment plans or cash for keys, and who decides? Judgment calls and owner involvement differ by company.
  • How do you verify income and what fraud do you actually see? Practical screening methods go beyond written policy.
  • How long do units sit vacant between tenants and what causes the delays? Vacancy time is a key owner metric with many local causes.
  • How do you manage vendors: who is reliable, how do you onboard them, and what happens when they are unavailable? Vendor networks decide response speed and quality.
  • What parts of compliance (licensing, registration, lead paint, deposits) are hardest to keep current? Shows where mistakes and exposure occur.
  • How do you handle voucher tenants, and how much delay does the housing authority cause? Process and delay vary greatly by authority.
  • What does your typical day or week look like, and what interrupts it most? Reveals real time allocation versus formal duties.
